Why noise matters: a stochastic view of cell fate.

Two genetically identical cells, sitting next to each other in the same dish, given the same concentration ofdrug, can do completely different things. One dies. One divides.

The problem with averages

When you train as a biologist, you start to think about pathways as arrows on a slide. A receptor activates a kinase. A kinase, in turn, phosphorylates a transcription factor. That transcription factor enters the nucleus. The natural mathematical object behind this picture is an ordinary differential equation where concentrations evolve smoothly, the system has a steady state, and given the same inputs, you always get the same outputs.

That picture is correct on average. The problem is that biology, at the level of single cells, is rarely on average.

Most molecules inside a cell exist in tens or hundreds of copies — small enough numbers that the next reaction is genuinely a probabilistic event, not a smooth average.

What stochastic models actually do

Stochastic chemical kinetics replaces the smooth flow of concentrations with a sequence of discrete reaction events. The Gillespie algorithm can be used to represent the signaling. At each step, given the current state, you ask "what's the probability that any reaction fires in the next tiny window of time?". 

# the loop, conceptually
for run in range(N):
    state = initial_state.copy()
    while t < T_max:
        dt, reaction = sample_next_event(state, rates)
        state = apply(state, reaction)
        t += dt
    record(state)

Where this changes the answer

In the lung-cancer work I did at Vanderbilt, the deterministic version of the model tells you that at high enough drug concentration, all cells stop dividing. The stochastic version gives you the insight that a small but real fraction of cells slip through the cell-cycle checkpoint anyway, and once they do, they can divide before apoptosis occurs.

If you'd like to play with a stochastic process, I built a small interactive demo that runs a two-state stochastic switch in your browser.

Further reading: Gillespie (1977), Exact stochastic simulation of coupled chemical reactions. Wilkinson, Stochastic Modelling for Systems Biology.
