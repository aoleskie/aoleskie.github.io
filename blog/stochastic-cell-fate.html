Why noise matters: a stochastic view of cell fate.

Two genetically identical cells, sitting next to each other in the same dish, given the same concentration of the same drug, can do completely different things. One dies. One divides.

The problem with averages

When you train as a biologist, you start to think about pathways as arrows on a slide. A receptor activates a kinase. The kinase phosphorylates a transcription factor. The transcription factor enters the nucleus. The natural way to write that down is an ordinary differential equation: concentrations evolve smoothly, the system settles into a steady state, and the same inputs always give you the same outputs.

That picture is correct on average. The problem is that biology, at the level of single cells, is rarely on average.

Most molecules inside a cell exist in tens or hundreds of copies — small enough numbers that the next reaction is genuinely a probabilistic event, not a smooth average.

What stochastic models actually do

Stochastic chemical kinetics replaces the smooth flow of concentrations with a sequence of discrete reaction events. The Gillespie algorithm does this exactly, without approximation. At each step you take the current state and ask which reaction fires next, and how long you wait for it. 

# the loop, conceptually
for run in range(N):
    state = initial_state.copy()
    while t < T_max:
        dt, reaction = sample_next_event(state, rates)
        state = apply(state, reaction)
        t += dt
    record(state)

Where this changes the answer

In the lung-cancer work I did at Vanderbilt, the deterministic version of the model says that at high enough drug concentration, all cells stop dividing. The stochastic version says a small but real fraction slip through the cell-cycle checkpoint anyway, and once they do, they can divide before apoptosis catches them.

The deterministic model answers a real question, and answers it well: where does the population end up on average. The stochastic model answers a different one. It tells you how many cells escape that average, and in a disease where the survivors are most of the problem, that's the number I care about.

If you'd like to play with a stochastic process, I built a small interactive demo that runs a two-state stochastic switch in your browser.

Further reading: Gillespie (1977), Exact stochastic simulation of coupled chemical reactions. Wilkinson, Stochastic Modelling for Systems Biology.
