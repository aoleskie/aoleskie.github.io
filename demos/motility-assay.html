The science.

The original reference is Schaller et al., Nature 2010 covering a high-density actin motility assay where actin filaments were propelled across a surface coated with myosin motors. At low density, the filaments wander around and occasionally align with their neighbors. As density is increased, they spontaneously organize into swarms, vortices, and traveling density waves. None of these patterns were programmed in. They emerge from local interactions between filaments that, on their own, know nothing about global structure.

The model running above is a continuous variant of the Vicsek model — the simplest setup that produces this kind of behavior. Each filament has a position and an orientation. At every step, it averages the orientations of its neighbors within a small radius, rotates partway toward that mean, adds a little noise, and moves forward at a constant speed. That's the entire rule set, and it's enough to recover most of what Schaller and colleagues observed.

One of the most interesting aspects of this model is its phase diagram. With density and alignment both high and noise low, the system locks into traveling bands. If your drop the density, the bands will break into mobile swarms. Drop alignment toward zero and the system disorders, regardless of density (which is the whole point of the No alignment preset). The order parameter readout (|⟨v⟩|/v₀) tracks how aligned the population is at any moment, and the phase tag in the readout panel uses a rough heuristic on density and order to put a name on what you're looking at.

Why this is here.

This was one of the first papers I read closely as a young researcher; I'm still finding threads of it in the work I do. I had started a project working with semiflexible polymer networks during graduate school, examining the local forces associated with an actin gradient, and although that line of research got cut short before it could really go anywhere, the underlying interest never went away.

What stuck with me from the Schaller paper, and from this whole family of active-matter problems, is the idea that there can be a higher level of description beyond the molecular one. You can know everything about a single filament and still not predict what a dish full of filaments will do. The collective behavior is its own object of study, with its own rules, and it depends on parameters that don't show up in any individual particle's equations. That observation, that systems sometimes have to be understood at the level where the pattern lives, became the throughline for much of what I've worked on since: stochastic modeling of cell-fate decisions in cancer, transcriptomic analyses, the kind of problems where averaging can obscure the underlying dynamics.

I built the simulator above as a way to revisit the original question with the tools I have now. It's small, it's in the browser, and it's not trying to be a research result. But every time I run it and see bands form out of nothing, I'm reminded of how weird and interesting system behavior can be.
