research · 01

Substrate stiffness as a tuning knob for therapeutic tolerance.

Non-small cell lung cancer cells respond to EGFR inhibitors very differently depending on the mechanical stiffness of the substrate they're growing on. Soft environments like healthy lung tissue tend to sensitize the cells; stiff environments like tumor stroma let some of them survive and keep dividing. The question I spent most of my postdoc on was why.

We built a stochastic, mechanistic model of the cell-fate decision in PySB, parameterized it with in-vitro measurements of mitotic time, quiescence, and death, and ran it across thousands of Gillespie trajectories per condition. A deterministic model of the same network misses what matters here; averaged over a population, the cells look uniformly suppressed by the drug. The stochastic version shows a small fraction slipping into a fast-dividing state and proliferating from there. Small, but enough to account for part of why the drug isn't curative on its own. 

# sketch: simulating one stiffness condition
from pysb.simulator import StochKitSimulator
sim = StochKitSimulator(model, tspan=tspan)
results = sim.run(
    initials=initials_from_stiffness(kPa=12.0),
    n_runs=2000,
)
trajectories = results.dataframe

research · 02

Co-expression networks on melanoma RNA-seq.

This was a collaboration with the Richmond and Quaranta labs on a study of CXCR2-driven melanoma. They had bulk RNA-seq from Braf/Pten tumors with and without Cxcr2 knockout, and wanted to know which transcriptional programs sit downstream of the receptor. My contribution was the WGCNA portion of the analysis: building gene co-expression modules from the tumor RNA-seq, ranking transcription factors by their centrality to each module, and testing which modules were significantly different between the wild-type and knockout conditions. The analysis served as orthogonal support for a TFCP2L1 finding the rest of the team had identified through differential expression. Published as part of Yang et al., Mol Cancer, 2023. 

# module detection, abridged
library(WGCNA)
sft <- pickSoftThreshold(expr, powerVector = 1:20)

  TOMType    = "signed",
  minModuleSize = 30,
  mergeCutHeight = 0.25

tools · 02

SignalScreen.

A Python pipeline that takes a biological target and creates a curated, scored list of candidate compounds. The first target I pointed it at was the retinoic acid receptor, specifically the γ subtype, which is the form most expressed in skin and a reasonable handle for capturing retinol-like effects with less of the irritation.

The pipeline itself is five stages, each writing a tidy intermediate to disk so the next stage can resume cleanly when an external API fails. The first two stages gather target metadata from Guide to Pharmacology and bioactivity records from ChEMBL. The third scores each ligand on RARγ potency and on selectivity against RARα and RARβ, using the pChEMBL values from the bioactivity table. The fourth takes the top hits and enriches them with PubChem identifiers, mechanisms of action, pathway associations from Reactome, clinical trials from ClinicalTrials.gov, and literature pulled from PubMed, BindingDB, and ChEMBL's own document records. The fifth writes a single summary TSV.

From around 975 candidates that came back from the ChEMBL pull, three ended up at the top of the list: CHEMBL3936922 (structurally close to trifarotene, with strong RARγ selectivity but very little literature or clinical history), CHEMBL330998 (closer in structure to retinoic acid itself, again with thin downstream evidence), and palovarotene (already approved for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, but with off-target effects that limit its appeal as a cosmetic-skincare lead).

The current pipeline is a proof of concept rather than a deployable tool. If I pick it back up, I'd expand the candidate pool with a structure-similarity search, weight the scoring function by the volume and quality of the supporting literature, and use the off-target data the pipeline already pulls to penalize compounds with bad selectivity profiles. The shape of the thing (pull the target, pull the ligands, score, enrich the top hits) is target-agnostic, so pointing it at a different receptor should let the same five stages run unchanged.

tools · 05

Polar filament patterns.

An in-browser, Vicsek-style simulator of a high-density actin motility assay, after Schaller et al., Nature 2010. Thousands of self-propelled filaments follow one local alignment rule, and depending on a handful of parameters you get moving swarms, traveling density waves, or nothing but disorder.

The Schaller paper was one of the first I read closely as a young researcher, and without realizing it at the time, I've been circling its questions ever since. I started a graduate-school project on actin networks asking related things about local forces inside an actin gradient. That line of work got cut short; the interest didn't. I built the simulator partly to revisit the original question with the tools I have now, and partly because it still produces the most striking emergent behavior I've ever watched come out of a few lines of code.
