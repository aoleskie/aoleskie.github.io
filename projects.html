research · 01

Substrate stiffness as a tuning knob for therapeutic tolerance.

Non-small cell lung cancer cells respond to EGFR inhibitors very differently depending on the mechanical stiffness of the substrate they're growing on. Soft environments like healthy lung tissue tend to sensitize the cells; stiff environments like tumor stroma let some of them survive and keep dividing. The question I spent most of my postdoc on was why.

The approach we employed was a stochastic, mechanistic model of the cell-fate decision built in PySB, parameterized with in-vitro measurements of mitotic time, quiescence, and death, and run across thousands of Gillespie trajectories per condition. Using a deterministic model of the same network we miss something important, averaged over a population, the cells looked uniformly suppressed by the drug. But the stochastic version shows that a small fraction are slipping into a fast-dividing state and proliferating from there. That fraction is small, but it's also a meaningful piece of why the drug isn't curative on its own.

research · 02

Co-expression networks on melanoma RNA-seq.

This was a collaboration with the Richmond and Quaranta labs on a study of CXCR2-driven melanoma. They had bulk RNA-seq from Braf/Pten tumors with and without Cxcr2 knockout, and wanted to know which transcriptional programs sit downstream of the receptor. My contribution was the WGCNA portion of the analysis: building gene co-expression modules from the tumor RNA-seq, ranking transcription factors by their centrality to each module, and testing which modules were significantly different between the wild-type and knockout conditions. The analysis served as orthogonal support for a TFCP2L1 finding the rest of the team had identified through differential expression. Published as part of Yang et al., Mol Cancer, 2023.

Biotech funding patterns.

A side project that started because I was curious how funding patterns affect the overall biotech job market. tools · 04

Polar filament patterns.

An in-browser, Vicsek-style simulator of a high-density actin motility assay, after Schaller et al., Nature 2010. Thousands of self-propelled filaments interacting through a simple local alignment rule showing the emergence of moving swarms, traveling density waves, or disorder, depending on a handful of parameters.

The Schaller paper was one of the first papers I read closely as a young researcher, and although I didn't realize it at the time, it shaped almost everything I've worked on since. I started a graduate-school project on actin networks that was asking related questions, examining local forces within an actin gradient, and although that line of research got cut short, the underlying interest never went away. I built the simulator partly to revisit the original question with the tools I have now, and partly because it still produces the most striking emergent behavior I've ever watched come out of a few lines of code.
